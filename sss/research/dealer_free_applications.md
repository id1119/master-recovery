# Dealer-Free / Distributed Key Generation and Modern Applications of Shamir Secret Sharing

> Historical research only (2026-08-15). No construction in this document is
> implemented by Master Recovery; see `../../SHAMIR_AUDIT.md`.

*A research survey of improvements built on Shamir's secret sharing (SSS), with protocol-level detail sufficient for implementation. Research only — no code in this document.*

**Baseline.** Shamir's (t, n)-threshold scheme (Shamir, *How to Share a Secret*, CACM 22(11):612–613, 1979, https://doi.org/10.1145/359168.359176): a dealer samples a polynomial f(x) = s + a₁x + ⋯ + a_t xᵗ over a finite field 𝔽_q (degree t = threshold − 1, constant term = secret s) and gives party P_i the point (i, f(i)). Any t+1 shares interpolate f and recover s = f(0); fewer than t shares give Shannon-zero information about s. Everything below removes the trusted dealer, adds verification, refreshes shares, or moves computation into the exponent.

**Common notation used throughout.** 𝔾 a prime-order group of order q with generator g (or additive elliptic-curve group with base point G); x-coordinates of shares are the party identifiers 1…n (or public non-zero field elements α₁…α_n); Lagrange coefficient λ_S,i(0) = ∏_{j∈S∖{i}} (0−j)/(i−j) over the reconstructing set S; "in the exponent" means the value g^a (or a·G) is published instead of a. Additive notation (EC) is used for group operations where it makes the algebra read naturally; both are the same thing.

## 1. Pedersen DKG (1991) — Distributed Key Generation via n parallel Feldman VSS executions

**Identity.** Torben Pryds Pedersen. The building blocks: P. Feldman, *A Practical Scheme for Non-Interactive Verifiable Secret Sharing*, FOCS 1987, https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/TOPICS/secretsharing/feldmanVSS.pdf; T.P. Pedersen, *Distributed Provers and Verifiable Secret Sharing Based on the Discrete Logarithm Problem*, DAIMI PB-388, Aarhus, 1991/1992, https://tidsskrift.dk/daimipb/article/view/6621, and the DKG analysis: R. Gennaro, S. Jarecki, H. Krawczyk, T. Rabin, *Secure Distributed Key Generation for Discrete-Log Based Cryptosystems*, J. Cryptology 20(1), 2007 (online 2006), https://doi.org/10.1007/s00145-006-0347-3. Pedersen's information-theoretically hiding *VSS* (not DKG) is in *Non-Interactive and Information-Theoretic Secure Verifiable Secret Sharing*, CRYPTO '91, LNCS 576, https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-46766-1_9.

**Summary — what it adds over plain Shamir.** Plain Shamir requires a trusted dealer who knows the whole secret. Pedersen's DKG distributes the *dealer's* role: every party P_i independently samples a random polynomial f_i (its own random "secret" z_i = f_i(0)), Feldman-verifiably shares it to everyone, and the *group secret* x = Σ z_i is never known to anyone; each party's share is s_i = Σ_j f_j(i). Feldman's commitments make every share individually checkable (VSS), so malicious dealers are detected, publicly disqualified (QUAL set), and their contributions excluded. Because Feldman's VSS also reveals each z_i in the exponent (y_i = g^{z_i}), the group public key y = g^x is computed publicly as the product of the y_i of non-disqualified parties — the DKG *outputs the public key* for free, which is exactly what threshold cryptosystems need. The core math is the linearity of Shamir: sums of shares of polynomials are shares of the summed polynomial, so "sharing without a dealer" = each party shares a random polynomial; the sum of the polynomials is a random polynomial whose constant term is the random shared secret.

**Protocol details (Feldman-based "Ped-DKG" / JF-DKG).**
Setup: public group (𝔾, q, g), identifiers 1..n, threshold t+1 out of n.
1. **Deal round (parallel Feldman VSS).** Each P_i samples f_i(x) = a_{i,0} + a_{i,1}x + ⋯ + a_{i,t}x^t ∈ 𝔽_q[x], sets z_i = a_{i,0}. For every j: send s_{ij} = f_i(j) to P_j over a private authenticated channel. Broadcast commitment vector (A_{i,0}, …, A_{i,t}) with A_{i,k} = g^{a_{i,k}}.
2. **Verification.** Each P_j checks, for every received share from dealer P_i: g^{s_{ij}} ≟ ∏_{k=0}^t A_{i,k}^{j^k} (in EC notation: s_{ij}·G ≟ Σ j^k·A_{i,k}). If the check fails for dealer i, P_j broadcasts a **complaint** (i, j, s_{ij}).
3. **Complaint resolution.** For each complaint, dealer P_i must broadcast the correct s_{ij}. If it fails to respond with a value passing the check, or responds with an invalid value, P_i is **disqualified**; it is removed from QUAL and its z_i is dropped (reconstruction of the final secret then ignores its contributions — the secret is only defined over QUAL).
4. **Output.** QUAL = set of parties whose dealing survived. Each P_i computes its long-term share s_i = Σ_{j∈QUAL} s_{ji}. Everyone computes the public key y = ∏_{j∈QUAL} A_{j,0} = g^{Σ z_j} = g^x, and the verification data A_k = ∏_{j∈QUAL} A_{j,k} (allows robust reconstruction: any party can check that revealed shares lie on the summed polynomial, or interpolate without trusting the revealers).
5. **Knowledge-of-secret requirement (for t ≥ n/2 use).** As noted in the FROST paper (§5.1) and the ZF FROST book, a party can choose its z_i *after* seeing others' commitments and shares (rogue-key attack in the dishonest-majority regime). Fix: each P_i appends a zero-knowledge proof of knowledge of a_{i,0} (Schnorr proof), so it cannot copy/adapt others' contributions.

**Cost comparison.** Per party: deals one degree-t polynomial → t+1 exponentiations for commitments, n private field elements sent; each receiver does t+1 exponentiations per share check (n such checks) → total O(nt) exponentiations per party, O(n²) field elements and O(n(t+1)) group elements of communication. One communication round (plus complaint rounds only when faults occur). Feldman's own VSS costs: 2 rounds, O(nk) communication, O((n log n + k)(nk log k)) computation per the FOCS '87 abstract — DKG multiplies that by n parties but parallelizes the rounds.

**Composability notes.** This is the substrate for nearly everything in this document: FROST's key generation (RFC 9591 Appendix C and ZF FROST's DKG), threshold Schnorr/EdDSA, ElGamal threshold decryption, and the nonce/randomizer generation in threshold DSS (GJKR). It composes with proactive refresh: the GJKR paper itself notes DKG is the natural engine for "generation of the refreshing polynomial in proactive secret sharing" — run a DKG on the zero secret (each party shares a random polynomial with constant term 0; see §4). It does **not** give a guarantee of a *uniformly distributed* output: GJKR prove JF-DKG (as originally specified) can be biased by an adversary who adaptively disqualifies parties after seeing their deals; they give a 3-round variant (New-DKG) with an initial Pedersen-VSS commitment phase that pins the secret before any dealer input can adapt, then a proof that JF-DKG is nonetheless fine for DL-based *signature* applications where only secrecy (not uniformity) is needed. Robust reconstruction works: the summed commitments A_k verify any claimed share. Works with Feldman's VSS by construction (that's all it is). PVSS is *not* used here (no verifiable encryption of shares needed — shares are private-channel, not public-key-encrypted).

**Implementation pitfalls.**
- *Degree consistency:* every dealer must use the same degree t and exactly t+1 commitment coefficients; a dealer emitting a higher-degree polynomial under a length-t commitment list breaks the summed-polynomial degree, and Lagrange interpolation then silently produces garbage. Reject on commitment count and check degree at verification.
- *The bias problem:* in the one-round version the last honest party to speak can be disqualified after the fact by a coalition; if you need guaranteed uniform output, use the GJKR 3-round (commit-then-deal) variant.
- *Complaint rounds are interactive:* a faulty dealer only needs to be *excluded*, but you must have a consistent view of QUAL (reliable broadcast of commitments and complaints, or they can be partitioned). The FROST/ZF docs stress: a "broadcast channel" here means *agreement on the same value*, not fan-out.
- *Knowledge proofs* are mandatory when t ≥ n/2; without them the scheme is vulnerable to rogue-key/key-race attacks where the adversary chooses its share after seeing yours.
- *Feldman hiding is computational:* y = g^x is revealed by design (fine for public keys — the public key is supposed to be public), but you cannot use this DKG to share secrets that must remain hidden in the exponent. Use Pedersen-VSS (two polynomials f, f′; commitments C_k = g^{a_k}h^{b_k} with h = g^c, c unknown) when the shared value itself must stay information-theoretically hidden.

## 2. JVRSS in FROST — Joint Verifiable Random Secret Sharing as Key Generation (Komlo–Goldberg 2020, RFC 9591)

**Identity.** C. Komlo, I. Goldberg, *FROST: Flexible Round-Optimized Schnorr Threshold Signatures*, SAC 2020, eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2020/852; IRTF RFC 9591, *The Flexible Round-Optimized Schnorr Threshold (FROST) Protocol for Two-Round Schnorr Signatures*, 2024, https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9591; implementation walkthrough: ZF FROST book, *Distributed Key Generation*, https://frost.zfnd.org/tutorial/dkg.html. The JVRSS name itself (Joint Verifiable Random Secret Sharing) appears in the threshold-ECDSA literature (GG18/GG20, §3) for the same construction.

**Summary — what it adds over plain Shamir.** FROST's key generation is Pedersen's DKG specialized to the Schnorr setting: the group key s = Σ z_i is Shamir-shared by *everyone* contributing a random polynomial (JVRSS), Feldman commitments make the sharing verifiable, and the group public key Y = s·G emerges as Σ Y_i. What FROST adds *beyond* the DKG is the signing half, which is where the SSS ideas do the real work: (i) the long-term key shares s_i = f(i) are used with Lagrange coefficients λ_i over the *participating* subset, so any t+1 of n can sign; (ii) per-signature nonces are generated by *additive* secret sharing (each participant contributes a random d_i; R = Σ D_i, no Lagrange needed) and *bound* to the message and commitment set to kill the Drijvers et al. / Wagner one-more-DL forgery attack that plagues concurrent threshold Schnorr; (iii) signature shares are verified against the public key shares Y_i before aggregation. Which SSS ideas carry over: polynomial interpolation and Lagrange coefficients (verbatim), Feldman's commitment verification of shares (verbatim), additive (degree-0) sharing for nonces (a degenerate case of Shamir), and the "sum of shares = share of sum" linearity for aggregation.

**Protocol details (RFC 9591 two-round signing; identifiers i ∈ [1, n], NonZeroScalar; hash functions H₁, H₂, H₃, domain-separated by contextString; group 𝔾 order q).**
*Key generation (dealer-free, per RFC 9591 §4 and ZF DKG part1–part3):*
1. Each P_i picks f_i(x) = a_{i,0} + a_{i,1}x + ⋯ + a_{i,t}x^t, sends f_i(j) privately to P_j, broadcasts commitments C_{i,k} = a_{i,k}·G for k = 0..t, plus (in the ZF variant) a zero-knowledge proof of knowledge of a_{i,0}.
2. P_j verifies each incoming share: f_i(j)·G ≟ Σ_k j^k·C_{i,k}. Failed checks → broadcast complaint, dealer must respond with the correct share or be dropped from QUAL.
3. Each P_i: secret share s_i = Σ_{j∈QUAL} f_j(i); public key share Y_i = s_i·G; group public key Y = Σ_{j∈QUAL} C_{j,0}; keeps Y_j for all j (share verification in signing).
*Round one (nonce commitment):* P_i samples two fresh nonces (d_i, e_i) ∈ 𝔽_q², publishes commitments (D_i = d_i·G, E_i = e_i·G). (Two nonces: one "hiding", one "binding".)
*Round two (share generation):* given message m, participant set S (|S| ≥ t+1), and commitment list com = {(D_j, E_j)}_{j∈S}: P_i computes binding factors ρ_i = H₁(i, m, com, Y) for each i∈S, the group commitment R = Σ_{j∈S} D_j + Σ_{j∈S} ρ_j·E_j, challenge c = H₂(R, Y, m), and its signature share z_i = d_i + ρ_i·e_i + λ_i·s_i·c where λ_i is the Lagrange coefficient for i over S evaluated at 0. It then erases (d_i, e_i).
*Aggregation (coordinator):* verify each z_j against Y_j (check z_j·G ≟ D_j + ρ_j·E_j + λ_j·c·Y_j), then z = Σ z_j; output (R, z) — a standard single-party Schnorr signature verifiable with Y. RFC 9591 specifies exact procedures: `nonce_generate`, `derive_interpolating_value` (Lagrange), `compute_challenge`, `verify_signature_share`, and the ciphersuites (ristretto255-SHA-512, P-256-SHA-256, Ed25519, Ed448, secp256k1 in the ZF implementation).

**Cost comparison.** DKG: one broadcast round + one unicast round, O(n²) field elements, O(nt) commitment points (dominated by the Feldman checks, n per party). Signing: 2 rounds regardless of n; each participant does 2 scalar mults + 1 Lagrange-weighted scalar mult; coordinator does |S| share-verifications (|S| multi-scalar mults, optimizable with MSM batching per Connolly–Gouvêa) + 1 aggregation. Compared to naive threshold Schnorr (e.g., Stinson–Langford-style t-out-of-n with n-way nonce resharing), FROST saves n−t nonce-sharing executions per signature and supports full concurrency safely.

**Composability notes.** DKG composes directly with proactive refresh (§4) — refresh by zero-secret DKG; the ZF book's three-part DKG maps to part1 broadcast / part2 encrypted unicast / part3 verify-and-derive. Robust reconstruction of the key exists only as a fallback (you shouldn't need the secret itself, only signatures). Composes with Feldman VSS, Pedersen VSS (for the commitment round, per GJKR), and — because signatures verify under plain Schnorr — with any single-party verifier. RFC 9591 explicitly does *not* do: deterministic nonces (fatal, see pitfalls), single-round signing without preprocessing (out of scope), and dealer-less *key* generation as a standardized procedure (DKG is left to the application; Appendix C gives trusted-dealer keygen).

**Implementation pitfalls.**
- *Nonce reuse is catastrophic:* deriving nonces deterministically, or reusing (d_i, e_i) across two messages with the same commitment set, yields two linear equations in (d_i, e_i, s_i) and completely recovers the private key share. RFC 9591 hammers this: "deriving nonces deterministically allows for a complete key-recovery attack". FROST nonces must be fresh uniform randomness per signature.
- *Binding-factor mistakes:* ρ_i must hash *all* participants' commitments, the message, and the group public key, with domain separation; omitting any input reopens the Drijvers et al. attack (adversary adaptively picks its commitment/challenge). Must also include its *own* identifier in the hash domain or identifiers can collide.
- *Consistent view of commitments:* participants must agree on the commitment list and the participant set S; the coordinator can't silently drop a commitment.
- *Lagrange over the actual S:* coefficients must be recomputed for every signing subset (they change when S changes) and must be derived from the *participant list* (RFC 9591 `derive_interpolating_value(participant_list, identifier)`), not cached from a previous session.
- *Validation:* deserialize/validate every scalar and group element (reject identity elements, non-canonical encodings, points off the curve); abort on any invalid signature share.
- *DKG encryption requirement:* the unicast shares in part2 must be encrypted (authenticated + confidential) — an eavesdropper who sees all shares can rebuild the key; the ZF book calls this out explicitly.

## 3. Threshold ECDSA / EdDSA — Shamir key shares + Lagrange interpolation of signatures

**Identity.** The blueprint: V. Shoup, *Practical Threshold Signatures*, EUROCRYPT 2000, LNCS 1807 (eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/1999/011) — RSA threshold signatures with non-interactive, robust share generation; see also the accessible treatment in the Primitives monograph on threshold cryptography, https://primitives.pub/distributed-systems/monographs/threshold-cryptography. Threshold Schnorr/EdDSA: FROST (RFC 9591, §2) and its ancestors (Schnorr threshold, Desmedt–Frankel, Langford, Stinson). Threshold ECDSA: Y. Lindell, *Fast Secure Two-Party ECDSA Signing*, CRYPTO 2017, eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2017/552; R. Gennaro, S. Goldfeder, *Fast Multiparty Threshold ECDSA with Fast Trustless Setup* (GG18), eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2018/987; R. Gennaro, S. Goldfeder, *One Round Threshold ECDSA with Identifiable Abort* (GG20), eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2020/540.

**Summary — what it adds over plain Shamir.** The signature value itself is a function of the secret key, so "threshold signing" replaces *reconstruction* with *computation on shares*. For Schnorr/EdDSA the trick is pure SSS linearity: a signature share z_i = λ_i·s_i·c + (nonce share) is produced by each signer using only its own share s_i and its Lagrange coefficient; the coordinator's z = Σ z_i equals a valid single-party signature because Σ λ_i·s_i = s. For RSA the same Lagrange trick must happen *in the exponent* (σ = x^d from ∏ σ_i^{λ_i} = x^{Σ λ_i d_i}), which is the "Shamir in the exponent" idea (§7) — Shoup's contribution is making interpolation well-defined over the integers (multiply everything by Δ = l! so Lagrange coefficients are integers) and adding per-share verification keys v^{s_i} that make signature shares publicly checkable (robustness, non-interactivity). For ECDSA the blocker is that the signature needs k⁻¹(x·r + m): inversion is non-linear, so the shares of x and of the nonce k must be *multiplied* without revealing anything — this is done with additively re-shared (t, t) sessions plus Paillier homomorphic encryption and zero-knowledge proofs (Lindell; GG18; GG20), while the *key generation* remains pure JVRSS/Shamir and the public key remains Y = Σ Y_i·(nothing) — Lagrange only enters the signing math.

**Protocol details.**
*Shoup 2000 (threshold RSA, Protocol 1, k = t+1):* dealer (or DKG — but RSA DKG needs the unknown φ(N), so a trusted dealer is typical): choose n = pq, p = 2p′+1, q = 2q′+1 strong primes, m = p′q′; public exponent e prime, e > l (l = #players); f(X) = d + a₁X + ⋯ + a_{k−1}X^{k−1} ∈ ℤ[X] mod m; share s_i = f(i) mod m; pick random v ∈ QR_n; verification keys VK = v, VK_i = v^{s_i} mod n. Signing on m: x = H(m); player i outputs share x_i = x^{2Δs_i} mod n (the 2Δ fixes the sign/parity issue so shares are in QR_n). Verify share: x_i^e ≟ (VK_i)^{2Δ}... precisely, check x_i^e ≡ x^{2Δ s_i} against the published v-keys: x_i^e = (x^{2Δ s_i})^e = x^{2Δ·e·s_i}, and with v^{s_i} known the check is that (x_i)^e equals the pairing-free quantity computed from the public key and v-keys via interpolation in the exponent (Shoup's eq. (7)-(8); the combiner interpolates ∏_{j∈S} x_j^{λ^S_{i,j}} mod n with integer coefficients λ^S_{i,j} = Δ·∏(i−j′)/∏(j−j′)). Final signature y = ∏_{j∈S} x_j^{2Δλ^S_{0,j}}... yielding y^e = x (standard "hash-and-invert" RSA signature). Protocol 2 (k ≥ t+1, random-oracle) uses s_i = f(i)⁻¹ mod m and u with Jacobi symbol 1 to detect misbehavior.
*Threshold Schnorr (FROST):* see §2 — z_i = d_i + ρ_i e_i + λ_i s_i c; R from additive nonce shares.
*Threshold ECDSA (GG20-style, the t-out-of-n standard):* keygen = JVRSS (each P_i shares f_i, s_i = Σ f_j(i), Y = Σ Y_i) with proofs of knowledge. Signing: each P_i additively re-shares its share s_i into a (t,t) representation over the *current* participant set S (SIP — share in the "partial" representation with Lagrange coefficient scaling: ŝ_i = λ_i·s_i; now Σ_{i∈S} ŝ_i = s exactly, additive). Jointly generate nonce shares k_i with commitments Γ_i = k_i·G; the group R = Σ k_i·G; every P_i publishes an encryption of its additively-shared contribution to the product (ŝ_i·k + ⋯) under Paillier, with ZK proofs that the ciphertext is well-formed and the plaintext in range; a combiner homomorphically evaluates k⁻¹·(s·r + m) mod q (using the shared k-reconstruction trick: k is known mod q only jointly, so the product protocol runs "in the exponent of the Paillier encryption"); each share or the final signature is verified with zero-knowledge; identified cheaters are excluded. Lindell's 2-party version is the same machinery restricted to n = 2. (Alternative non-Paillier routes: Lindell–Nof, CMP/Canetti et al., Damgård's "full threshold ECDSA" via homomorphic commitments.)

**Cost comparison.** Shoup: each share = 1 modular exponentiation (2Δs_i exponent); verify = 1 exponentiation; combine = k exponentiations; total ≈ (k+2) exp per signature, completely non-interactive (the whole point for use inside asynchronous Byzantine agreement). FROST: 2 rounds, ~3 scalar mults + 1 hash per signer, |S| verifications at the combiner. GG20: 1 message per party per round, but each message carries Paillier ciphertexts + ZK proofs (kilobytes per party; the ZK range proofs dominate); keygen is the cheap part. Threshold ECDSA remains ~1–2 orders of magnitude heavier than threshold Schnorr.

**Composability notes.** Threshold Schnorr/EdDSA: full pipeline (dealerless DKG + refresh + robust signing) is standard (FROST/ZF). Shoup-style threshold RSA: robust (share verification), non-interactive, composes with proactive refresh only in the specialized proactive-RSA literature (Frankel–Gemmell–MacKenzie–Yung, Rabin '98) — generic Shamir refresh breaks because the interpolation domain is ℤ_{φ(N)}-mod-m over the integers and φ(N) is secret; that's why Shoup's shares are integer-valued. Threshold ECDSA: keygen (JVRSS) composes with Feldman VSS; signing composes with verifiable secret redistribution for SIP only if each party's re-share is Feldman-committed; GG20 gives identifiable abort (exclusion, not just abort); works with proactive refresh of the long-term shares (§4) as long as the *refresh itself* is verifiable.

**Implementation pitfalls.**
- *RSA interpolation over ℤₘ:* you cannot invert mod m (m is secret), hence Δ = l! scaling; forgetting the Δ factor (or the 2Δ in the exponent) yields shares not in QR_n and verification failures. e must be a prime > l; moduli must be strong primes for the QR argument.
- *Nonce inversion in ECDSA:* k must never be revealed mod q; k⁻¹ is computed *jointly* (via the additive/Paillier machinery), and k must be uniformly random mod q — a biased or reused k leaks x (the classic ECDSA nonce attack applies verbatim to thresholds; GG20/GG18 discuss the birthday bound on nonce reuse across many signing sessions).
- *Paillier correctness proofs:* without range/well-formedness ZK proofs, a corrupted party can encrypt garbage that shifts the signature and with enough runs leaks the key (GG18's proofs were tightened in follow-ups; the one-round GG20 fixes known attacks — do not implement the naive version).
- *Rogue-key attacks:* JVRSS keygen needs proof-of-knowledge of each z_i when t ≥ n/2; this is the same pitfall as §1.
- *Lagrange with the right set:* for additive (t,t) SIP sessions, coefficients must be scaled by the Lagrange coefficient over S before adding, else the session secret is not s.
- *Adaptive-corruption subtleties:* plain Shamir key shares are not adaptively secure without erasures/commitments; "Born and raised distributively" (LJY16, https://marcjoye.github.io/papers/LJY16borndist.pdf) shows Pedersen-DKG-derived keys are adaptively secure for its scheme but this is delicate — prefer a proof-followed construction over improvisation.

## 4. Proactive Refresh / Dealer-Free DKG Refresh — zero polynomials and mobile adversaries

**Identity.** A. Herzberg, S. Jarecki, H. Krawczyk, M. Yung, *Proactive Secret Sharing or: How to Cope with Perpetual Leakage*, CRYPTO '95, https://research.google/pubs/proactive-secret-sharing-or-how-to-cope-with-perpetual-leakage/; A. Herzberg, M. Jakobsson, S. Jarecki, H. Krawczyk, *Proactive Public Key and Signature Systems*, ACM CCS '97, https://research.google/pubs/proactive-public-key-and-signature-systems/; model origin: R. Ostrovsky, M. Yung, *How to Withstand Mobile Virus Attacks*, CRYPTO '91 (cf. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proactive_secret_sharing). Asynchronous instantiations: Cachin–Kursawe–Lysyanskaya–Strobl, CCS 2002 (below in §5); B. Przydatek, R. Strobl, *Asynchronous Proactive Cryptosystems Without Agreement*, SCN 2004, LNCS 3329, https://crypto-test.ethz.ch/publications/files/PrzStr04.pdf; C.U. Günther, S. Das, E. Kokoris-Kogias, *Practical Asynchronous Proactive Secret Sharing and Key Refresh*, eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2022/1586. (Note on "Rabin et al. 1999 *Efficient asynchronous proactive SSS*": no such paper could be confirmed — the closest Rabin item is T. Rabin, *A Simplified Approach to Threshold and Proactive RSA*, CRYPTO '98; the asynchronous proactive line actually runs Herzberg et al. '95 → Cachin et al. '02 → Przydatek–Strobl '04 → Günther et al. '22.)

**Summary — what it adds over plain Shamir.** Plain Shamir is only secure against an adversary who corrupts < t+1 parties *for the lifetime of the secret*; a mobile adversary that eventually visits every server breaks it. Proactive secret sharing (PSS) adds a periodic **refresh**: every party deals a *random polynomial with constant term zero* (a "zero polynomial" δ_i, δ_i(0) = 0) verifiably to all others, and each party adds the received evaluations to its old share: s_i′ = s_i + Σ_j δ_j(i). By linearity the secret f(0) is unchanged (Σ δ_j(0) = 0), but the sharing is a fresh random polynomial — old shares are information-theoretically useless. This is *refresh without a dealer* because the dealers are all parties sharing zero, and it is exactly a DKG for the zero secret. Recovery of lost/corrupted shares is dealer-free too: the injured party re-shares its share (or the community reconstructs it via VSS) and everyone's share is restored. The adversarial bound becomes "at most t corruptions *within a single refresh period*" — security over time, not just at any instant.

**Protocol details (Herzberg et al., Feldman-based version).**
Periodic update protocol, per phase τ:
1. Each P_i samples δ_i(x) = δ_{i,1}x + δ_{i,2}x² + ⋯ + δ_{i,t}x^t (degree t, zero constant term), computes u_{i,j} = δ_i(j) for all j, sends u_{i,j} to P_j privately, broadcasts commitments B_{i,k} = g^{δ_{i,k}} (k = 1..t).
2. P_j checks g^{u_{i,j}} ≟ ∏_{k=1}^t B_{i,k}^{j^k}; on failure, broadcasts complaint (i, j, u_{i,j}); dealer responds with the correct value or is excluded from this update (and then the honest parties must compensate: an excluded dealer's δ_i is publicly reconstructed from t+1 correct complaints — note *this* is why the commitment values must be for a zero-constant polynomial: anyone can then evaluate δ_i(j) for any j and finish the update without the dealer).
3. Each P_i sets s_i^{(τ+1)} = s_i^{(τ)} + Σ_{j∈QUAL} u_{j,i}, and *erases* all data from phase τ (and deletes s_i^{(τ)}).
Recovery protocol: a party that lost its share (or was corrupted-then-restored) has its share re-shared by the community: each P_i shares its share s_i via a (t+1)-of-n VSS of the *same* polynomial evaluation? — precisely: the community runs VSS on the value f(i) (each honest party's share s_i = f(i) is itself a secret; the helpers VSS-share f(i) among all, the recovering party interpolates f(i) = Σ over helpers of Lagrange·(their shared f(i)) and adds δ-type updates to match the current phase). With Pedersen VSS instead of Feldman, secrecy becomes information-theoretic (the price: computational binding, so the dealer of a bad sharing can be caught only if DLP is hard).
Refreshing public keys in proactive *signature* systems (CCS '97) additionally requires renewing the communication keys (sign/encrypt channels) — otherwise a mobile adversary that steals a server's comms key can impersonate it forever; the paper maintains the PKE/signature key-pair of every server proactively as part of the same protocol.

**Cost comparison.** Refresh: n VSS executions (one per party as dealer) → O(n²) private field elements, O(n·t) commitment group elements, O(n²) messages total, 1 round + complaint resolution rounds; per party O(nt) exponentiations (dominated by share checks). Compare re-running a DKG for a fresh key: identical cost but *changes the secret* — so PSS refresh is the only way to keep a persistent key (threshold signatures/decryption) proactively secure. Asynchronous instantiations cost more: CKLS '02 refresh is O(n³) messages / O(κn⁵) communication with optimal resilience n > 3t; Günther et al. '22 achieve O(n³) communication (and O(cn²) with a sortition-selected committee of size c), with reconstruction threshold f+2 ≤ d ≤ 2f+1 for n = 3f+1 — the +1 comes from the adversary knowing the extra point p(0) = 0 on the zero polynomial.

**Composability notes.** Refresh composes with: Feldman VSS and Pedersen VSS (two variants given in Herzberg et al.); DKG protocols (refresh = DKG of zero — literally "run a new instance of Pedersen's DKG where the shared secret is (0,0) and add the resulting shares", as documented in the LJY16 adaptive-threshold-signature paper); threshold change (choose the update polynomial of a different degree — Herzberg et al. '95 §5.3 gives change-of-threshold via the renewal polynomial; see also §6); recovery (share re-sharing via VSS, robust reconstruction of the refreshed secret still via summed Feldman commitments); asynchronous settings via CKLS/APSS/Przydatek–Strobl; dynamic committees (hand-over to a *new* set of parties — Levrat–Rambaud–Urban, *Breaking the t < n/3 Consensus Bound: Asynchronous Dynamic Proactive Secret Sharing under Honest Majority*, eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2022/619). PSS does *not* compose with non-erasable storage: if the adversary can read old and new shares simultaneously, refresh provides nothing.

**Implementation pitfalls.**
- *Erase or die:* old shares and old randomness must be provably erased; without erasures the security guarantee degrades to plain threshold. Systems that cannot erase (e.g., append-only logs) must not use PSS.
- *Zero-constant discipline:* every refresh polynomial must have constant term exactly 0 and degree exactly t; a "refresh" with δ(0) ≠ 0 silently changes the secret and signs with the wrong key.
- *Excluded-dealer compensation:* you cannot simply drop a dealer from the refresh — the zero polynomial must still be summed for everyone's share to stay consistent; publicly reconstruct the excluded dealer's δ_i from t+1 validated complaints.
- *Complaint consistency:* like DKG, requires a reliable broadcast view of commitments and complaints, else the update splits the group into inconsistent sharings.
- *Synchronization:* in partially synchronous deployments, all parties must enter a phase before any old share is erased — a party that refreshes early with a stale view erases data others still need; the CKLS phase-timer model (adversary-scheduled local timers, messages not delivered within a phase are lost) is the clean way to model this.
- *Asynchronous threshold inflation:* zero-polynomial APSS yields d ≥ f+2 (not f+1) — plan for the reconstruction threshold one above the corruption bound (ARSS, which re-randomizes the *secret* too, recovers d = f+1 at the cost of a fresh key).

## 5. Asynchronous SSS / AVSS — weak vs strong commitment, echo-based agreement

**Identity.** C. Cachin, K. Kursawe, A. Lysyanskaya, R. Strobl, *Asynchronous Verifiable Secret Sharing and Proactive Cryptosystems*, ACM CCS 2002, eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2002/134; C. Cachin, K. Kursawe, F. Petzold, V. Shoup, *Secure and Efficient Asynchronous Broadcast Protocols*, eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2001/006; V. Shoup, *Lightweight Asynchronous Verifiable Secret Sharing with Optimal Resilience* (JOC 2024), eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/536; I. Abraham, G. Asharov, S. Patil, A. Patra, *Asymptotically Free Broadcast in Constant Expected Time via Packed VSS*, TCC 2022, eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2022/1266, and *Bingo: Adaptivity and Asynchrony in VSS and DKG*, eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2022/1759; I. Abraham, G. Asharov, S. Patil, A. Patra, *Perfect Asynchronous MPC with Linear Communication Overhead*, EUROCRYPT 2024, eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/432. (Lineage: Ben-Or–Goldwasser–Wigderson 1988 first AVSS; Canetti–Rabin 1993 first polynomial-time AVSS — both impractical; CKLS '02 made it practical.)

**Summary — what it adds over plain Shamir.** In an asynchronous network (adversary-controlled message scheduling, no timeout possible, no reliable broadcast available), plain Shamir sharing is unusable: a dealer can send inconsistent shares to different parties, and parties cannot wait for all n messages. AVSS adds (i) *verifiability* — Pedersen-style commitments let each receiver check its share non-interactively, and (ii) *agreement on the sharing* — echo/ready message patterns (the Bracha reliable-broadcast skeleton) ensure that if any honest party accepts a sharing, all honest parties eventually do, even though the dealer may be Byzantine. The honest-majority constraint sharpens: with no synchrony, resilience drops to t < n/3 (consensus impossibility), and every AVSS must be *complete*: once one honest party terminates, all honest parties can reconstruct. The CKLS construction is the workhorse (used, e.g., by HoneyBadgerBFT for its asynchronous DKG): the dealer shares via a *bivariate polynomial* so that consistency between any two receivers is checkable from their respective evaluations, and a Bracha-style echo/ready phase replaces broadcast. The **weak vs strong commitment** distinction: a *weakly* committed sharing guarantees honest parties can reconstruct *some* consistent secret but the adversary may later cause a different value to be reconstructed; *strong* commitment pins a unique value at share time. Asynchronous settings mostly need weak commitment (the value is committed when t+1 honest parties hold correct shares); strong commitment is what synchronous VSS gives for free and costs extra rounds or assumptions asynchronously.

**Protocol details (CKLS AVSS, computational, DLP-based).**
Setup: 𝔾 of order q, generators g, h (log_g h unknown). Dealer D with secret s wants an (n, k, t) dual-threshold sharing (reconstruct with k, safe against t; for threshold use n > 3t, k = t+1).
1. D picks a symmetric bivariate polynomial F(x, y) ∈ 𝔽_q[x, y], degree t in each variable, F(0,0) = s, and random G(x, y) (blinding), computes commitments C_{i,j} = g^{F(i,j)}·h^{G(i,j)} for 0 ≤ i, j ≤ t, broadcasts them (one (t+1)² matrix).
2. For each receiver j: D privately sends the two row/column polynomials f_j(x) = F(x, j), f̄_j(x) = G(x, j) (and symmetric copies for the other direction).
3. **Echo round:** P_j checks consistency of its own polynomials with the commitments (verify g^{f_j(i)}h^{f̄_j(i)} ≟ C_{i,j} for all i — the symmetry F(i,j) = F(j,i) is what makes this work), then sends to every other P_l the two values f_j(l), f̄_j(l) ("echo").
4. **Verify/ready:** P_l checks, for each echo from P_j, that it matches *its own* f_l(j), f̄_l(j) (i.e., f_j(l) ≟ f_l(j) — bivariate symmetry again) and that the commitment check passes; collects valid echos; when ≥ k valid echos (or ready messages) arrive, broadcasts READY; on ≥ t+1 READYs (or ≥ n−t echos) it accepts/terminates the sharing.
5. **Reconstruction:** parties send their f_i values; any k parties' shares reconstruct F and s = F(0,0), with the commitments enabling robust filtering of bad shares (evaluate the committed polynomial at the claimed points).
The protocol has the exact communication pattern of Bracha's broadcast (two async rounds), achieving agreement and completeness with n > 3t. The DKG application (e.g., in HoneyBadgerBFT): every party runs AVSS as dealer of a random z_i; each party sums accepted shares → JVRSS done asynchronously.

**Cost comparison.** CKLS AVSS: message complexity O(n²), communication O(κn³) (κ = security parameter), optimal resilience n > 3t, constant rounds. Versus the previous AVSSs (BGW '88, Canetti–Rabin '93): O(n⁵)/O(n⁶ log n) messages and O(n⁶)/O(n⁸ log n) communication — a Θ(n³)–Θ(n⁴) improvement. Shoup '23 (lightweight): shares over rings ℤ_{p^k} and fields, *hash-function-only* (no DLP), optimal resilience t < n/3, batching with amortized linear communication on the happy path (O(n) per batched secret), complete (honest-share ⇒ all-honest-shares). Abraham et al.: packed VSS/broadcast with O(nL + n⁴ log n) for a length-L broadcast (2022/1266); Bingo PAVSS with O(λn²) words to share f+1 secrets and adaptively secure ADKG in O(λn³) expected words; perfect async MPC with linear communication (2024/432) using trivariate polynomials.

**Composability notes.** AVSS composes with: DKG (async JVRSS — HB-BFT style); proactive refresh in async networks (CKLS §5: refresh via n AVSS of zero-polynomial shares + multivalued Byzantine agreement, O(n³) messages / O(κn⁵) communication; Günther et al. '22 APSS improves this to O(n³)/O(cn²) communication and drops the extra agreement round via the existing common coin); Byzantine agreement (Canetti–Rabin instantiated with CKLS AVSS becomes the first *practical* asynchronous BA — this is literally a headline result of CKLS '02); atomic broadcast (CKPS '01, which also contributed the consistent-broadcast-with-proof primitive reused by later AVSSs). Weak-commitment AVSS composes with MPC (the linear-communication async MPC of '24/432 is built on a rate-1 *weak binding* secret sharing). It does *not* compose with plain synchronous VSS assumptions: timeouts cannot be used to "assume the dealer failed"; and strong-commitment needs extra machinery (e.g., combining AVSS with an external value-validated agreement) if uniqueness is required.

**Implementation pitfalls.**
- *Symmetric bivariate discipline:* F must be exactly symmetric (F(i,j) = F(j,i)); the dealer must send *both* the row and column polynomials, and every consistency check (echo vs own share; commitment vs echo) is an instantiation of that symmetry. A non-symmetric F is undetectable at the dealer's end but breaks reconstruction.
- *Termination thresholds:* "wait for n−t echos, or t+1 ready" — hardcoding "wait for everyone" deadlocks in asynchrony; hardcoding too few breaks agreement. Use exactly the n > 3t arithmetic from the paper.
- *Consistent commitment view:* commitments must be *broadcast* (echoed themselves, in CKLS the dealer's broadcast is implemented by the same echo mechanism or a reliable-broadcast wrapper); a receiver that verifies against a different commitment matrix has no guarantee.
- *Randomness and secrecy:* the blinding polynomial G is essential — without it the bivariate values leak through the commitments; never reuse (F, G) across sessions; derive per-session IDs (CKLS tag IDs) to prevent replay/cross-protocol confusion.
- *Weak vs strong commitment confusion:* with only a weak binding AVSS you cannot claim "the secret is committed at share time" — for applications like common coins that's fine, for unique-value needs you must layer agreement.
- *Adaptive corruption:* BGW/Canetti–Rabin-style AVSS was adaptively insecure in the practical constructions; Bingo (2022/1759) exists precisely to fix adaptivity at the same asymptotics — pick it (or prove your own) for adversarial models that corrupt *after* seeing protocol transcripts.

## 6. Verifiable Re-sharing and Share Conversion — changing threshold/scheme without reconstructing

**Identity.** The classic technique: Y. Desmedt, S. Jarecki, *Redundant Sharing: A New Approach to the Problem of the Reconstruction of Secret Shares*, 1993 (widely cited in threshold-cryptography literature; see also the Wikipedia treatment of threshold change during renewal, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proactive_secret_sharing, and the survey at https://primitives.pub/distributed-systems/monographs/threshold-cryptography). Formal conversion theory: R. Cramer, I. Damgård, Y. Ishai, *Share Conversion, Black-Box Secret Sharing, and Linearly Secure Computation*, TCC 2005 (with the more recent *New Results in Share Conversion*, eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/1781). Dealer-free, non-interactive threshold change: Harn et al., threshold-changeable secret sharing from pairwise keys / symmetric bivariate polynomials (cf. the application writeup in *Editable Blockchain Scheme Based on Threshold Changeable Secret Sharing*, https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/125999544.pdf). GJKR 2006 (https://doi.org/10.1007/s00145-006-0347-3) also contains the relevant DKG-based re-sharing ("Run DKG with a zero/known secret").

**Summary — what it adds over plain Shamir.** Three related problems, one algebraic answer. (1) *Re-sharing to the same threshold (proactive refresh):* each holder re-shares *its share* f(i) via a fresh degree-t polynomial h_i with h_i(0) = f(i); the new share of P_j is g(j) = Σ_i λ_i·h_i(j) over the t+1 contributing old holders — because Σ λ_i·f(i) = f(0) = s, the new sharing g has g(0) = s and the *secret never appears in the clear*. (2) *Changing the threshold* t → t′: re-share with polynomials of degree t′ instead of t; if t′ > t, more holders must contribute (the combining set must still span f). (3) *Share conversion between different linear schemes* (Cramer–Damgård–Ishai): each holder applies a fixed *local* function to its share, and the outputs are a valid sharing of the same secret under a target scheme — Shamir → additive, additive → Shamir, CNF → anything, etc. The dealer-free, non-interactive threshold change (Harn et al.) uses *pairwise keys*: with a symmetric bivariate polynomial f(x, y) = f(y, x), parties i, j both compute the pairwise key k_{i,j} = f(ID_i, ID_j); to move threshold t → t′, each party broadcasts v_i = (its secret contribution) + Σ (selected pairwise keys); the sum Σ v_i cancels all pairwise keys, revealing the target linear combination of secrets — no interaction beyond the broadcasts, no party ever reconstructs anything.

**Protocol details (verifiable redistribution, t-of-n → t′-of-n′).**
Input: old holders {P_i}, each with share s_i = f(i) on degree-t polynomial f (secret s = f(0)); Feldman commitments B_k = g^{a_k} known (from DKG/VSS); new threshold t′.
1. Each old holder P_i: sample h_i(x) = s_i + b_{i,1}x + ⋯ + b_{i,t′}x^{t′} (degree t′, h_i(0) = s_i = its *own share* — the re-shared value is the share, not the secret); send h_i(j) to new holder P_j (or to same set); broadcast commitments D_{i,k} = g^{b_{i,k}}.
2. P_j verifies every received h_i(j): g^{h_i(j)} ≟ g^{s_i}·∏_{k=1}^{t′} D_{i,k}^{j^k} — note this needs g^{s_i} = B-interpolation, i.e., g^{s_i} = ∏_{k=0}^t B_k^{i^k}, which is public; complaints handled as in §1 (dealer responds with the correct value or is excluded; excluded dealers' h_i is publicly reconstructed).
3. P_j computes its new share g(j) = Σ_{i∈A} λ_i^A(0)·h_i(j), where A ⊆ {old holders} with |A| = t+1 (or the t+1 with valid shares) and λ_i^A(0) are the Lagrange coefficients for evaluating f(0) from A. Then g(0) = Σ λ_i s_i = s. Verify: the public counterpart g(j)·G = Σ λ_i·h_i(j)·G is computable from the broadcast data, so robustness extends to the new sharing; new commitments for g are Σ λ_i·(shifted) D-combinations — everyone can derive g's coefficient commitments in the exponent, enabling future robust reconstruction without a new dealer.
If t′ < t (decrease threshold): any t′+1 of the *new* shares suffice because g has degree t′; no holder set change needed. If t′ > t: require ≥ t′+1 old holders to contribute so that the coefficient set spans f's degree.
**Cramer–Damgård–Ishai conversion (Shamir → additive, the case FROST-style SIP uses):** each P_i with share f(i) on degree-t f over S (|S| = k ≥ t+1) computes ŝ_i = λ_i^S(0)·f(i); then Σ_{i∈S} ŝ_i = f(0) = s with *no interaction* — a (t,t)-of-|S| additive sharing of the same secret. This is the exact step GG20/Lindell use per signing session, and it is the degenerate case of the redistribution above with t′ = 0.

**Cost comparison.** Redistribution: t+1 dealers × (n′ private field elements + (t′+1) commitments + t+1 exponentiations per verification) → O((t+1)·n′) field elements, O((t+1)(t′+1)) commitments, one round (+ complaint rounds). Share conversion (Shamir→additive, CDI): **zero communication** — purely local Lagrange scaling. Harn threshold change: n broadcasts, O(n²) pairwise key derivations, local arithmetic only. Versus reconstruction-then-redeal: the whole point is the secret is never assembled (reconstruction is a single point of catastrophic exposure and requires t+1 honest parties at once).

**Composability notes.** Re-sharing composes with: proactive refresh (same protocol with t′ = t; Herzberg et al. '95 is the synchronous special case, §4); Feldman/Pedersen VSS (commitments make it verifiable); DKG (a DKG run with all-zero constant terms *is* a refresh — the "DKG of zero" idiom); threshold-changeable storage/blockchain chameleon-hash applications (Harn et al. line); evolving access structures (2024/1781 generalizes conversions to evolving schemes — some conversions become impossible, so check convertibility before relying on it). It does *not* compose blindly with *different fields* or *different groups*: the conversion is only defined between linear schemes over the same domain (CDI 2005; vector-space isomorphisms à la eprint 2020/691 §7 can bridge different groups via a common DL structure). PVSS composes (Schoenmakers' PVSS is a Shamir-in-exponent sharing where shares are verifiably encrypted, and re-sharing of such sharings is itself publicly verifiable).

**Implementation pitfalls.**
- *Contributing-set mismatch:* the Lagrange coefficients must be over the exact set A of contributing old holders, and A must be agreed by all new holders — different A's give different (but equally valid) new sharings; if new holders disagree on A, they hold inconsistent sharings.
- *Scale factors in non-field domains:* over ℤ_{p^k} or rings, Lagrange denominators may be non-invertible; the Desmedt–Jarecki/Shoup approach multiplies by Δ = l! and accepts Δ-scaled secrets; forgetting this yields fractions and silent corruption. (Shoup '23's AVSS shows how to keep shares integral over ℤ_{p^k}.)
- *Degree of the re-share polynomial:* using the old degree t when the new threshold is t′ is only correct if t′ = t; a degree mismatch makes the new sharing either under-determined (t′ > t: fewer than t′+1 new shares reconstruct nothing... actually they reconstruct the degree-t′ polynomial but it doesn't match f) or over-determined (redundant but compatible).
- *Verification needs public s_i·G:* redistribution assumes the *share* values' public counterparts g^{s_i} are computable (true if the old sharing came with Feldman commitments, as in DKG); for plain uncommitted Shamir you cannot verifiably redistribute.
- *Non-interactive threshold change ≠ robust:* Harn's pairwise-key scheme is elegant but assumes honest behavior and does not handle a corrupt broadcaster; add commitments or accept the weaker model.

## 7. Secret Sharing on Elliptic Curves / "Shamir in the Exponent" — public evaluation and homomorphism

**Identity.** Origin: P. Feldman, FOCS 1987 (already cited, https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/TOPICS/secretsharing/feldmanVSS.pdf) — the commitments A_k = g^{a_k} are exactly the polynomial "in the exponent". Rigorous modern treatments: Y.-H. Chen, Y. Lindell, *Feldman's Verifiable Secret Sharing for a Dishonest Majority*, eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/031; *Improved Threshold Signatures, Proactive Secret Sharing, and Input Certification from LSS Isomorphisms*, eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2020/691 (general vector-space "MPC in the exponent" framework); KZG polynomial commitments: A. Kate, G. Zaverucha, I. Goldberg, *Constant-Size Commitments to Polynomials and Their Applications*, ASIACRYPT 2010, eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2010/215; threshold isogeny/CSIDH instantiation: L. De Feo, M. Meyer, *Threshold Schemes from Isogeny Assumptions*, PKC 2020, eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2019/1288; PVSS: B. Schoenmakers, *A Simple Publicly Verifiable Secret Sharing Scheme and its Application to Electronic Voting*, EUROCRYPT 1999 (https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-48910-X_11).

**Summary — what it adds over plain Shamir.** Shamir's algebra is linear over a field; publish each coefficient *multiplied by a group generator* (in the exponent: A_k = g^{a_k}, on EC: A_k = a_k·G) and the polynomial becomes *publicly evaluable and publicly verifiable* while staying computationally hidden: anyone can compute f(x)·G = Σ x^k·A_k without knowing f, and every share holder's claim s_i = f(i) is checkable by a single group equality. This single move buys: (i) VSS (Feldman); (ii) DKG public-key output (y = g^x emerges from the sum of A_0's, §1); (iii) *public evaluation* — Lagrange interpolation in the exponent (Σ λ_j·(share_j·G) = s·G, the public key from public key shares; ∏ x_j^{λ_j} = σ for multiplicative schemes like RSA, §3); (iv) polynomial commitments (KZG: commit to f with a single group element in a bilinear group, and prove any evaluation with a constant-size proof — the modern generalization); (v) homomorphic operations on shares (additions and scalar multiplications of shares are local; the exponent version of Shamir is additively homomorphic in the secret), which underlies PVSS (anyone verifies that encrypted shares lie on the committed polynomial) and "MPC in the exponent" for threshold decryption, attribute-based encryption, and more. The EC instantiation is what makes all of this *fast* (fast group operations, 256-bit fields, efficient multi-scalar multiplication) — Shamir over EC points is the same field arithmetic as over a prime field, with the generator multiplication doing the hiding.

**Protocol details (Feldman-on-EC, per Chen–Lindell 2024 — the dishonest-majority-correct version).**
Group 𝔾 of prime order q, base point G, identifiers α_1…α_n ∈ 𝔽_q ∖ {0} distinct. Dealer shares s with degree d = t−1.
1. Round 1: set b_0 = s, sample b_1…b_{t−1} ← 𝔽_q, define s(x) = Σ_{k=0}^{t−1} b_k x^k; compute B_k = b_k·G for all k; send P_j the share s_j = s(α_j) privately; broadcast (B_0, …, B_{t−1}).
2. Echo/verification round (needed in the dishonest-majority / async world): every party re-broadcasts the commitment vector it received (echo); P_j aborts unless it saw the same vector everywhere; then P_j checks s_j·G ≟ Σ_{k=0}^{t−1} α_j^k·B_k. If it passes for all honest parties, all honest parties hold consistent shares on one committed polynomial.
3. Reconstruction / public evaluation: any party, given k claimed shares, evaluates the polynomial *in the exponent*: s·G = Σ_{i∈S} λ_i^S(0)·(s_i·G); a claimed share s_i is valid iff s_i·G matches the interpolation at α_i — robust reconstruction without ever seeing the secret.
KZG upgrade (for proof-carrying evaluation): commit as C = Σ b_k·(τ^k·G) (powers-of-τ SRS), and prove an evaluation via the quotient polynomial — one group element + one pairing check per evaluation, constant-size.
PVSS (Schoenmakers, exponent variant): dealer broadcasts B_k = g^{a_k} and for each party an ElGamal encryption (E_i = g^{r_i}, F_i = y_i^{r_i}·g^{s_i}) of its share; *anyone* verifies consistency by checking the Lagrange-interpolated product relation ∏ (E_i^{λ_i-set-eval}) vs. the B_k's at the α_i — publicly verifiable, no private channels needed.

**Cost comparison.** Feldman-on-EC: t group-multiplications to commit (plus n·t for n shares verification — or batched: one multi-scalar mult of size n·t per party with ~5× speedup). Public key from shares: one t-scalar mult (or MSM). KZG: constant-size commitments and proofs regardless of degree, but requires a trusted setup for the SRS (universal variants exist) and pairings (2–3× slower group). PVSS: n encryptions + n verifications, O(n) group ops on top of Feldman. Compared to plain Shamir over 𝔽_q: every operation costs one group-scalar mult instead of a field mult (roughly 1–2 orders of magnitude in wall-clock, still microseconds on modern hardware), and everything becomes publicly verifiable — the price of removing the trusted dealer and enabling public keys.

**Composability notes.** In-the-exponent sharing is the *lingua franca* of the whole survey: it is Feldman VSS (§1), JVRSS/FROST (§2), Shoup's RSA interpolation (§3), the DKG-of-zero refresh (§4), CKLS AVSS commitments and PVSS (§5), and KZG-style evaluation proofs. It composes with proactive refresh (publicly verify refreshed shares in the exponent), robust reconstruction (interpolate in the exponent), PVSS (add verifiable encryption), and threshold schemes over *any* group where the DL problem is hard (including CSIDH isogeny groups, 2019/1288, where "in the exponent" becomes "under the group action" — the threshold CSI-FiSh signature runs at ~single-party speed). Caveat: the exponent version *reveals g^s* by design — it is only appropriate when the secret's public counterpart is meant to be public (public keys), or when the secret is a hard-core-derived value; it cannot be used to hide a secret whose exponent must stay hidden (use Pedersen's two-polynomial VSS for that).

**Implementation pitfalls.**
- *Small-subgroup / validation:* group elements must be validated (on-curve, correct subgroup/order) or the share checks silently pass for invalid values; on Ed25519-style curves use the prime-order subgroup (e.g., ristretto255 encoding as RFC 9591 does).
- *The revealed-exponent leak:* using Feldman-style shares for a *secret that must stay secret* (e.g., a decryption key you don't want published as a public key) is a design error — the commitment leaks g^s. Pedersen's scheme exists for that case; mixing the two (Feldman for keys, Pedersen for secrets) is the standard split.
- *Degree vs. commitment length:* verification evaluates Σ_{k=0}^{t−1} α_j^k B_k — if the commitment vector has a different length than the polynomial's degree, checks pass on the wrong polynomial; enforce equality.
- *Interpolation in the exponent over wrong sets:* Lagrange coefficients must match the exact share set used; mixing coefficients from different sets gives garbage public keys (this bit real systems — see the FROST pitfalls).
- *Batch verification:* use multi-scalar multiplication and simultaneous exponentiation for the n share-checks; naive per-share double-and-add is the #1 performance footgun in practice.
- *No pairings needed for Feldman:* only for KZG-style proofs; don't force a pairing-friendly curve when Feldman suffices (pairing curves are slower and less analyzed for plain threshold signatures).

## 8. Composable Modern SSS Libraries and Protocols — hash-based VSS, SPDZ-preprocessing Shamir, HSS

**Identity.** Hash-based/lightweight VSS: V. Shoup, *Lightweight AVSS* (JOC 2024), eprint https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/536. SPDZ family: I. Damgård, V. Pastro, N. Smart, S. **Summary — what it adds over plain Shamir.** These are the *engineering* layer: they package the theoretical improvements above into composable protocols and frameworks, and add two more ideas plain Shamir lacks. (i) **Hash-based VSS** (Shoup '23): replace DL-based commitments (Feldman/Pedersen) with plain hash commitments in the random-oracle model, so VSS works over rings (ℤ_{p^k}) and fields alike, uses only hash evaluations (no group operations), batches many secrets with amortized linear communication, and tolerates t < n/3 asynchronously with completeness. (ii) **Preprocessing/offline-online MPC**: Shamir is *multiplicative* (local multiplication of shares gives a degree-2t sharing of the product), so the SPDZ-style split — an offline phase producing correlated randomness (Beaver multiplication triples, random bits, daBits/edaBits) and an online phase doing field arithmetic — turns Shamir into a general MPC engine; honest-majority variants generate triples *with Shamir itself* (Gennaro–Rabin–Rabin, Beerliová–Hirt's hyper-invertible matrices, Damgård–Nielsen), and malicious security comes from Mac-tags (SPDZ), triple sacrifice (Lindell–Nof; Chida et al.), or VSS-based checks. (iii) **HSS**: a *dealer* (not a dealer-set) splits a function input into n shares such that each server computes an *evaluation share* of f(x), and one output is assembled additively — "homomorphic SSS" extends Shamir's linear homomorphism to arbitrary functions, with security from DDH; ve-HSS adds public verifiability of the servers' evaluation shares, so a client can detect and pinpoint a lying server without recomputing f.

**Protocol details.**
*Hash-based AVSS (Shoup '23, sketch):* share via Shamir over 𝔽_q (or ℤ_{p^k}); the dealer commits to the share list with a Merkle-like/random-oracle hash and broadcasts; parties echo; the *completeness* gadget: if any honest party has its share, it is guaranteed all honest parties receive theirs (the "prayer"/wait-n−t discipline of async VSS); batching packs many secrets per polynomial so amortized communication is O(n) per secret on the happy path; no group, no DL assumption — only the hash. Verifiability is *computational* (hash collisions), hiding is computational; this is the cheapest VSS in existence at scale and is what modern blockchains use for DKG under asynchrony.
*Shamir MPC with preprocessing (honest majority, t < n/2):* share inputs via degree-t Shamir. Offline: generate multiplication triples ([a], [b], [ab]) — with hyper-invertible matrices, t+1 honest parties' random rows suffice to generate and verify O(n) triples at once (Beerliová–Hirt: linear communication per multiplication). Online: addition/scalar-mult local; multiplication: compute [a]+[x], [b]+[y] openly, then [xy] = (x+a)(y+b) − (y+b)[a] − (x+a)[b] + [ab] (Beaver); degree doubling handled by the resharing built into triple generation. Malicious checks: Mac-tag every share with a global secret α (SPDZ: share (x_i, γ_i) with γ = αx; opening a value reveals x, γ and every party checks; forgery detectable with probability 1 − 1/q), or triple-sacrifice (check two triples against a random linear combination before use; doubles the offline cost). MP-SPDZ packages ~34 variants (Shamir, replicated, SPDZ, SPDZ2k, TinyOT, garbling…) behind one interface with a Python DSL.
*HSS (BGI '16, DDH):* client keygen outputs (pk, ek); any input x is split into shares x_i (additive over 𝔽_2^m or 𝔽_p); each server i runs the *same* program Eval(pk, i, x_i) and outputs an evaluation share; the client combines (adds, or uses a final share) to get f(x). Reconstruction is additive; each server sees only its input share. For boolean circuits, DDH-based constructions handle *branching programs*; for arithmetic, LWE-based variants handle low-degree polynomials; ve-HSS appends a succinct proof per evaluation share so the client can verify and identify cheaters even if *all* servers are malicious.

**Cost comparison.** Hash-based VSS: O(n²) hash ops + O(n) communication per batched secret (happy path) vs CKLS's O(κn³); no group ops at all — 1–3 orders cheaper in CPU than Feldman-in-exponent at the same n. SPDZ: offline dominates (SHE/OT-based triple generation); online is field arithmetic + MACs with O(n) communication per gate — the honest-majority Shamir+Beaver online phase is the cheapest *maliciously secure* general MPC known (linear communication, no public-key ops). HSS: client work is cheap (share + evaluate n servers), server work equals the cost of evaluating f on shares; communication for the *result* is O(1) per output — compare: general MPC on the same function would require O(n) or more.

**Composability notes.** All of these compose with everything in this survey: hash-based VSS plugs into JVRSS/ADKG and async Byzantine agreement (it's the recommended VSS layer for modern DKG); Shamir-based MPC composes with DKG (inputs can be DKG-generated keys — threshold cryptography *is* MPC), with proactive refresh (refresh the triple/input sharings with zero-DKG), with robust reconstruction (VSS-committed shares), and with PVSS; HSS composes with threshold *schemes in the exponent* (the ve-HSS paper's multiplicative reconstruction is literally interpolation in the exponent of g₁) and with PIR/private-info-retrieval-style applications. Watch for *model mismatches*: hash-based VSS is ROM-only (Shoup's proof), SPDZ's MAC-check requires a global secret α that must be generated securely (a DKG!), and HSS's semi-honest-to-malicious gap is closed only by ve-HSS-style proofs.

**Implementation pitfalls.**
- *MAC-key generation:* SPDZ-style Macs are useless if the adversary learns α — generate α jointly (DKG) or via preprocessing; this is a classic silent-failure point.
- *Triple exhaustion/sacrifice:* consumed triples cannot be reused (reusing a triple is catastrophic); post-sacrifice (Lindell–Nof) requires storing all openings until the check — implement the buffer or the online phase is insecure.
- *Resharing degree growth:* local multiplication of two degree-t sharings gives degree-2t; every multiplication must go through the resharing/degree-reduction step or the threshold silently doubles — the single most common MPC implementation bug.
- *ROM reliance:* hash-based VSS proofs need a random oracle; in implementations, use domain-separated, collision-resistant hashes with per-session IDs, and don't reuse the oracle across contexts.
- *Batch/parallel correctness:* batching in Shoup's AVSS and SPDZ's offline phase assumes identical session semantics; interleaving sessions without unique IDs corrupts the commitment binding.
- *HSS share generation:* the dealer must *erase* the input after splitting (or the "at least one honest server" guarantee is vacuous); evaluation shares must be bound to the program (hash the program into the evaluation, or servers can swap outputs).
- *Framework lock-in:* MP-SPDZ's per-protocol preprocessing files (e.g., `Player-Data/…/Triples-p-P1`) are format-coupled; mixing offline data between protocol variants silently breaks security — regenerate per protocol.

## Synthesis: the variant list

1. **Feldman VSS (1987)** — Shamir + polynomial in the exponent; non-interactive share verification, robust reconstruction, computational hiding.
2. **Pedersen VSS (1991)** — two-polynomial commitment (g^{a_k}h^{b_k}); information-theoretic hiding, computational binding; used as the DKG commitment round.
3. **Pedersen DKG / JF-DKG (1991)** — n parallel Feldman VSS executions; secret = sum of dealer secrets; QUAL-based disqualification; public key from commitments.
4. **GJKR New-DKG (2006)** — 3-round DKG with Pedersen-VSS commitment phase; guaranteed uniform output; full simulation proof.
5. **JVRSS / FROST DKG (2020, RFC 9591 2024)** — Pedersen DKG + knowledge proofs + binding-factor nonce scheme; threshold Schnorr/EdDSA; 2-round signing.
6. **Shoup threshold RSA (2000)** — Δ = l! integer Lagrange interpolation in the exponent; non-interactive robust shares; v^{s_i} verification keys.
7. **Threshold ECDSA (Lindell 2017; GG18 2018; GG20 2020)** — JVRSS keygen + additive SIP + Paillier-based joint inversion with ZK proofs; identifiable abort.
8. **Proactive SSS (Herzberg–Jarecki–Krawczyk–Yung 1995; CCS '97)** — periodic zero-polynomial refresh + VSS-based share recovery; mobile-adversary security.
9. **Asynchronous proactive refresh (CKLS 2002; Przydatek–Strobl 2004; Günther–Das–Kokoris-Kogias 2022)** — dealer-free refresh under asynchrony; O(n³)/O(cn²) communication; ARSS variant.
10. **CKLS AVSS (2002)** — bivariate-polynomial sharing + Bracha echo/ready; O(n²) messages; n > 3t; the practical async VSS.
11. **Lightweight/hash-based AVSS (Shoup 2023/JOC 2024)** — hash-only VSS over rings; batching; amortized linear communication; optimal resilience + completeness.
12. **Abraham–Asharov–Patil–Patra AVSS line (2022–2024)** — packed VSS / PAVSS / Bingo; linear or near-linear communication; adaptive security; async DKG + MPC.
13. **Verifiable re-sharing / redistribution (Desmedt–Jarecki 1993)** — re-share shares, Lagrange-combine, change threshold t → t′ without reconstruction.
14. **Share conversion (Cramer–Damgård–Ishai 2005; 2024 follow-ups)** — local deterministic conversion between linear schemes; Shamir → additive is zero-communication.
15. **Dealer-free non-interactive threshold change (Harn et al.)** — pairwise keys from symmetric bivariate polynomials; broadcasts cancel keys.
16. **Shamir in the exponent / MPC on EC (Feldman 1987 → Chen–Lindell 2024)** — public evaluation, Lagrange-in-exponent public keys, homomorphic shares; KZG commitments; isogeny/CSIDH instantiations.
17. **PVSS (Schoenmakers 1999)** — publicly verifiable encrypted shares via exponent interpolation + ElGamal.
18. **SPDZ-family preprocessing MPC (DPSZ 2012; MASCOT 2016; Overdrive 2018; MP-SPDZ 2020)** — offline triples + Mac-tags; Shamir honest-majority variants with sacrifice; general composable MPC.
19. **HSS / ve-HSS (BGI 2016; 2025)** — homomorphic secret sharing with additive reconstruction; verifiable evaluation against all-malicious servers.

**Cross-cutting pitfalls, once more.** (a) Degree consistency is the #1 correctness failure across every protocol here — a single wrong degree silently invalidates Lagrange interpolation. (b) Nonce/randomness reuse is a key-recovery event in every threshold signature (§2, §3). (c) Binding-vs-hiding: Feldman leaks the exponent (fine for keys, fatal for secrets); Pedersen hides perfectly but binding rests on log_g h being unknown. (d) Round counts and complaint resolution assume broadcast-with-agreement, not multicast. (e) Under asynchrony, thresholds shift (t < n/3; d ≥ f+2 in APSS) and strong commitment is not free. (f) Erasure discipline is required for proactive security. All variants above interoperate because they are the same linear algebra — Shamir shares are always points on a polynomial over a field — with commitments, proofs, and rounds layered on top.
